My first question for us is: if AI can think for us, are we still learning? This is actually an open question. When I've asked developers this, some say it assumes we're already learning without AI. Others say we're learning how to use AI.

That's interesting. I think a lot of us are learning right now. A friend told me that for the last couple of months he's been figuring things out by playing with AI a lot. There's a lot of stuff with AI that you can learn. So yes, absolutely. I think there's something going on there.

Another answer I hear is that AI accelerates learning by summarizing books or breaking them into chunkable segments, so you can actually gain something rather than trying to consume a whole book at once. And another: maybe we are still learning, but at a different level.

My answer to this question is that I think we're actually under-investing in human learning today. And by extension, in human making. I'll explain why there's that extension. The reason I'm asking this question is that technology models have diverged from human learning since the advent of AI. That's my hypothesis, and I'll get into it.

Why do I think that? My claim is that we've seen a divergence between human learning and machine learning. And if they're diverging now, I also have to claim that at some point they were much closer together.

If you've looked at the history, or at researchers in the learning sciences, you'll see that each generation borrows its mental model of the mind from the latest technology. The steam engine, the web, the internet, and now AI. We're always trying to map how we think about our brains to the newest technology.

You can see a very clear pattern right now. Working memory? That's RAM. Long-term memory? That's writing to disk or to a database. The patterns we see in the world become schemas, and we organize data in databases in very particular ways. It's like a menu: when we look at one, we know the drinks are down at the bottom. These human mental models apply pretty directly to the way we build software, which has been the most current technology.

To drive this point home: the mental models of software and human learning match extremely well. Probably better than ever before. With the steam engine, people thought the brain worked like a steam engine, which was kind of a stretch. With software, there's a lot of parallel.

Here's another question. If models of human learning and software match, how did machines begin to organize information differently, in a way that made human minds and AI diverge? Let that sit for a second.

A related question: why can't we, as humans, become experts overnight? One answer I hear is that part of it is physiological.
